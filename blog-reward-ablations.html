What Offline Ablations Reveal About Agentic Tool-Use Rewards

We scored fixed policies across 200 git episodes under six reward variants. The numbers are offline. They show why scoring tool responses erases the learning signal GRPO needs, and why a small write-tool bonus is decisive.

◆

This is not a training curve. We did not re-train Qwen3-4B under each ablation. We scored fixed policies on a fixed episode set under six reward configs (A0-A5) and one buffer setting (A6). That is enough to say something sharp about reward design, and not enough to claim a new trained model.

The earlier training story still stands: we ran agentic RL on git tool use, watched rewards saturate at 0.96, fixed a lying reward, trained for hundreds of steps, and hit a W-shaped collapse. The offline numbers below explain why that first reward was fatal, and which fix actually creates a learnable signal.

The environment, briefly

The model gets a natural-language goal and 13 git tools. repo_path is injected server-side. Read episodes score whether the final message contains the right extracted values. Write episodes score whether the repo ends in the right state. Sandboxes are seeded and disposable.

Result 1: A3 erases the perfect-vs-empty signal

On read episodes, compare two policies:

  • perfect: call tools, then report the real values in the final message
  • empty final: call tools, then say almost nothing useful

A3 does not merely over-credit lazy rollouts. It makes the good policy and the lazy policy look almost the same. Under GRPO, advantages are (r_i - mean) / std within a group. If perfect and empty both score ~0.99, there is nothing left to reinforce. That is the offline explanation of the original failure mode: the reward was already saturated before training started.

The 0.10 gap is small in absolute terms and large in GRPO terms. It is enough to prefer "attempt a write tool" over "only inspect." Remove it (A1), and that preference disappears.

If stuck write attempts score around 0.1-0.4, a hard threshold of 0.1 throws most of them out before the model can climb. That matches the buffer-drain failure mode from training.

A3 is catastrophic: over-credits tool responses, almost no within-group variance, then post-batch filters drop every rollout until the job dies.

A1 is milder: training finishes, but zero-advantage is higher and effective batch is smaller than A0, with slightly less write-tool use. That lines up with the offline result that the elevated write-tool bonus is a small but real bootstrap signal.

A0 remains the healthiest short-run recipe among the three.

What this does not claim

  • These live runs are short (50 steps) and not a full re-train of the original 300-step experiment
  • No claim that a new checkpoint improved from offline scores alone
  • No claim that filesystem transfer already works for a trained policy

The offline numbers explain the failure modes. The live runs above confirm the ordering: A3 saturates and dies, A1 trains weakly, A0 stays healthiest.

What is next
